## What is going on in college athletics?
A commentary on how NIL is changing college athletics with an emphasis on sports economic concerns. 

## Introduction 
The world of college athletics is changing. With headlines of conferences suing schools for recruiting professional league drop outs, competitive balance has gone out of wack. This commentary of a tired Clemson Tigers fan delves into the pivotal moment of a Supreme Court case that created economic concerns of competitive balance disruption regarding player compensation. 

## Alston vs. NCAA
Shawne Alston, an ex-college football player, was the lead plaintiff in a pivotal court case involving antitrust accusations and the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA). The general overview of the case is that college athletes play for their colleges, generating illustrious revenue for the university and receiving merely tuition, education funds, and room and board as payment in these deals. The NCAA, at the time, had rigid restrictions on types of compensation that universities were able to provide athletes. The Supreme Court upheld a ruling stating that the NCAA would limit compensation to strictly education related payments. However, soon after that case, the NCAA changed their tune to allow student athletes to receive payments in exchange for their NIL, or Name, Image, and Likeness. The Supreme Court ruled that the NCAA was in fact in violation of the Sherman Act antitrust act meaning that the NCAA did not have grounds to "maintain amateurism" by purposely undercompensating players. The decision on the Alston case laid the foundation for future challenges to the NCAA’s adjustments regarding compensation. Long story short, now in 2026, the NCAA has ruled to allow players to get paid and receive endorsement deals from universities which is upsetting the competitive balance of the entire system. 

## NIL
NIL stands for Name, Image and Likeness. The NCAA describes NIL as “student-athletes [receiving] compensation from third parties for use of their name, image and likeness (NIL), such as social media posts, brand appearances or promoting products and services” (NCAA, 2026). The NCAA website provides information on how student-athletes in all divisions can participate as well as the guidelines they must follow according to the association rules.

## Current Situation
Clemson University's "I Pay Ten A Year" Booster club that definitely collects more than $10 per year from each of the booster club participants.
But where is the money coming from? All student-athletes are students first and not university employees so student tuition is not the largest contributor to student athlete contracts. Some funds towards student athletes is provided by the student body as a mandatory athletic fee placed in students tuition bills. However, most of the money that student athletes receive through an NIL system is from booster clubs and brand endorsements. These payments are an issue, similar to the reasoning behind the MLB creating regulation to control the payments on rookie contracts, college athletics is now facing the same predicament. Schools with less money, less robust booster clubs and overall less business resources to aid athletes to find NIL deals are now plagued with the short straw of disrupted competitive balance.
The transfer portal also plays a role in this process allowing athletes to “renegotiate” their salary every single year further disrupting competitive balance. One solution I think would be beneficial would be some sort of collective bargaining system for college athletes. A collective bargaining agreement is a contract that outlines the terms of employment between an employer and employee, in sports, the league owners and the players. Major professional leagues use these agreements to contract on subjects including revenue sharing, salary caps, contract terms and free agency. This solution is unlikely in the collegiate realm given the caveat that there are so many athletes in college sports and at the end of the day the student athletes are students first so the employer-employee relationship goes out the window. I don’t know what this means for college sports but it will definitely be interesting to see what happens next.

Anyways, Go Tigers!
