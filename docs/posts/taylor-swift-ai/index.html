Does Taylor Swift Sound Like AI, or Does AI Sound Like Taylor Swift?

By Elana Wong on Sep 30, 2026, 4 min read

AIcopyrightmusic industryintellectual property

The Backlash

Taylor Swift released new music, nothing new. What is new, however, is that it was poorly received by haters and fans alike. Social media users are comparing many parts of the song to a viral AI-generated song about metal coils (Notopoulos, 2025). One user said it sounds “exactly” like the coil song and that it was driving them insane (LaCroix, 2026). The similarity is subjective, and there is no indication the two songs are actually connected. One fan made a sharper point. When AI music generators were rising, Swift was the biggest thing in the world and unavoidable, so her music saturated the training data. On that reading, she isn’t copying AI. AI learned from her.

Songs That Sound Like Other Songs

One song resembling another isn’t new. Take Stella Lefty, whose song “Boston” drew criticism for sounding so much like Noah Kahan’s hit “Stick Season” that she added Kahan to the writing credits as inspiration (Moss, 2026). A human artist heard the criticism, acknowledged the influence, and gave credit. However, at this time, we have not seen AI companies following suit.

The Record Labels Are Suing

Universal Music and Sony Music recently filed a new lawsuit against Suno, an AI music generator, alleging the company used an “enormous” amount of their copyrighted music to train its AI model. Suno lets users type in lyrics, a melody, a genre, or themes, and the platform generates a song that they can refine, extend, or regenerate (Suno, 2026). They claim more than 60,000 songs were taken, and that the real number could be in the millions. Now around a half-dozen lawsuits are now pending against the company. The labels frame the case around a basic purpose of copyright. As the suit puts it, copyright has for centuries existed to incentivize human invention and imagination, now in danger of getting lost in the wake of generative AI (Nordstrom, 2026).

How Warner handled it differently. Warner Music Group settled with Suno and struck what both companies called a first-of-its-kind licensing partnership. Under the deal, artists and songwriters get control over whether and how their names, images, likenesses, voices, and compositions are used in AI-generated music (Stassen, 2025). Suno calls the Warner partnership a “blueprint” for how AI and the music industry can work together.

Swift’s Contribution to AI Model Building

Swift’s catalog is enormous, according to Genius:

  • 1,847 songs, counting soundtracks, Taylor’s Versions, and every acoustic, live, “From the Vault,” and deluxe version, plus poems and prologues.
  • 194 albums, counting digital, vinyl, TV, foreign-language, deluxe, and karaoke versions.
  • A 24-year career, with music released from 2002 to 2026.

She has contributed a huge amount to the music industry, and so, inevitably, to the data used to teach machines what music sounds like. A model trained on the most popular music of the last two decades will have absorbed a lot of Swift’s portfolio without providing any sort of credit.

Intellectual Property and the Economics

Why does this matter economically? Intellectual property rights exist so that creating things pays. If AI companies can ingest creative work without paying, the incentive that copyright was built to protect starts to erode. The Department of Justice has argued that copyright suits against AI companies should be widely dismissed. The New York Times sued OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, alleging that they evaded the paywall and downloaded articles illegally (Reisner, 2026). AI companies generally defend themselves by arguing they transform the work “enough” to create something new and original. Whether that holds up when a generated song resembles an existing one is the question courts are now being asked.

What Happens Next

In a true free market, businesses pay for the inputs they need. As Fox News contributor Mike Davis put it, “AI companies can and must do the same.” Warner’s deal with Suno suggests one path: licensing, with artists opting in and getting paid. Universal and Sony are exploring alternative options for fair compensation.

So how will the DOJ pursue ensuring AI companies pay for the content they scrape and build models from? And does Taylor Swift truly sound like AI, or is AI so heavily trained on her that its music sounds like hers? I am not entirely sure what will happen next, but I am curious to see what happens next.

Happy listening!
